StudyRoute: speaker notes for the 10-minute presentation
20 slides, 1,241 words: about 8.3 minutes at 150 words a minute (9.5 at a slow 130). The organisers' limit is 10 minutes. About half a minute a slide: keep moving. The anticipated Q&A appendix is separate and is not read aloud.
Say it in your own words; the notes are a guide, not a script to read.
==============================================================================

SLIDE 1: StudyRoute   (about 59 words, 24 seconds; running total 0:24)

Good morning. We are team TeamEkxin, and this is StudyRoute, our Topic C entry: a sandbox AI agent that helps a parent in Bangladesh pay tuition abroad. Everything is synthetic, no real money moves, and it runs live at studyroute dot bot-keep dot xyz. Our rule: the engine calculates, the AI explains, the human approves, every step is logged.

SLIDE 2: The posted fee is not the price   (about 61 words, 24 seconds; running total 0:48)

Parents compare banks by the fee they are shown. In our synthetic example, for ten thousand pounds with Provider B, 63 percent of the real cost is the hidden exchange-rate margin. Correspondent banks can deduct fees on the way, so the university may be paid short; the deadline spans two calendars; and the paperwork is unclear. Those five problems shape StudyRoute.

SLIDE 3: A family carries the risk   (about 61 words, 24 seconds; running total 1:12)

Two people feel this. The parent pays in taka, compares by the posted fee, and approves a large transfer with little to check it against. The student needs the university paid on time: a short or unmatched payment means late fees and weeks of waiting. StudyRoute gives both one plan; the parent decides, and the student can see but cannot approve.

SLIDE 4: How money really moves   (about 60 words, 24 seconds; running total 1:36)

This is the real chain: taka to an authorised dealer bank, a SWIFT instruction, a correspondent bank that may take a fee, a receiving bank that may take another, and the university matching by reference. Each step fails in a typical way, shown in red. We simulate the chain with invented banks and a double-entry ledger; no real money moves.

SLIDE 5: From question to settled payment   (about 63 words, 25 seconds; running total 2:01)

This is task completion. The parent types one sentence; the agent makes the quote, ranks providers, plans the deadline, checks KYC, and builds this plan card from engine output. A person creates the draft and approves with a fresh MFA code. The agent blocks the payment until KYC, allowlist and caps pass, then tracks it to settled. It never creates a payment itself.

SLIDE 6: Live: comparing providers   (about 61 words, 24 seconds; running total 2:25)

This is the public site, studyroute dot bot-keep dot xyz, opened on the 19th: ten thousand pounds of UK tuition. The ranking is the one our test T01 asserts: Provider B at 1.58 percent, Provider C at 1.63 with a warning that the university would be 35 pounds short, Provider A at 2.16. The engine calculates; the screen only shows it.

SLIDE 7: The engine does the maths   (about 57 words, 23 seconds; running total 2:48)

Every number comes from a deterministic engine: integer money, no floats. In our synthetic example Provider B is first at 1.58 percent. Provider C looks cheap but is quoted as SHA, so the university would receive 35 pounds less. Provider A is third at 2.16. The same engine drives the send plan and the currency stress test.

SLIDE 8: Live: one sentence, read back   (about 62 words, 25 seconds; running total 3:13)

On the live site the parent types one sentence. The trained reader only proposes; plain code turns the words into values; and the assistant must read them back before it plans. Only after a yes does it plan. Beside it, the deadline planner works backwards: send by 22 October. The assistant cannot approve; only the parent can, with a fresh MFA code.

SLIDE 9: An AI that cannot cheat   (about 57 words, 23 seconds; running total 3:36)

The assistant follows a fixed pipeline: policy check, engine facts, masking of personal data, an optional model, a number guard, a tool guard and the audit log. Any figure the model invents is rejected, then a template answers. So the AI can only propose: it cannot approve, submit or cancel, and it works with no API key.

SLIDE 10: Trained agents, measured   (about 62 words, 25 seconds; running total 4:01)

The assistant has a trained part, and we measured it, but the envelope matters more than the benchmark. A small model reads the intent and marks amounts, currencies and dates. A reinforcement-learning policy decides when to ask, read back or plan. A third rewords the engine's answer, every rewrite checked in code; a fourth scores each payment for a person to review.

SLIDE 11: Models against baselines   (about 67 words, 27 seconds; running total 4:28)

The same numbers as a graph. Left: the trained reader against our old rules on 113 messages written after it was frozen. Top right: the risk router's top one percent holds 63.2% of flagged payments; random order finds 1.2%. Bottom right: the dialogue policy cut wrong plans from 2.8% to 0.8% on simulated parents. Small samples, and as a plain yes or no the router is modest.

SLIDE 12: Risk score, human decision   (about 59 words, 24 seconds; running total 4:52)

Every payment gets an AI risk score out of 100 from four factors: amount, frequency, timeline and person. Low means no warning and nobody waits. Medium or high shows a warning and sends the payment to a person at the bank, who answers yes, ask for more, or no, with MFA. The score never approves; the parent still authorizes.

SLIDE 13: Proven, and not proven yet   (about 65 words, 26 seconds; running total 5:18)

What we have shown: one engine for every number, a human-only approval, an audit log that fails Verify when one row is edited, a Docker run, and attack probes defended. What we have not shown: rates and costs are invented, no real parent has used it, there was no outside penetration test, and most Bangladesh Bank figures are placeholders. Both lists are in the repository.

SLIDE 14: Every test we ran   (about 59 words, 24 seconds; running total 5:42)

Everything we ran: 1,679 backend tests, one skipped on Windows; 463 web tests; 24 browser tests; all 24 scenarios mapped; 314 security probe records. On the right is GitHub's own page for our latest commit: all six CI jobs green. These are our own tests; a fresh clone runs 1,672 of 1,673, and no outside party has tested it.

SLIDE 15: Security you can verify   (about 66 words, 26 seconds; running total 6:08)

Security and compliance is thirty percent of the score, so we made it checkable. Permission tiers are enforced in the domain layer, not just the screens. Only a human parent with a fresh MFA proof, bound to that payment, can approve; the AI has no approve, cancel or limit permission. On the right, the live audit log says PASS, and one edited row makes it fail.

SLIDE 16: Attacking our own app   (about 74 words, 30 seconds; running total 6:38)

We attacked our own application with six tools: access control, browser and XSS, planted secrets, hostile prompts, man-in-the-middle and replay, and login guessing. 314 records, all pass or informational. The man-in-the-middle test is the honest one: altered approvals, swapped MFA proofs, a stolen token and replayed webhooks were refused. But on plain HTTP a listener reads the password and token, and a stolen token can cancel a draft. We do not call that safe.

SLIDE 17: Security problems we found   (about 71 words, 28 seconds; running total 7:06)

We list our security problems because hiding them would cost more. On plain HTTP a listener reads the password and token; the public site uses HTTPS with HSTS, but nobody else has tested that. A stolen token can read payments and cancel a draft, not approve. We fixed account guessing and a telemetry leak. Old document rows stay readable, development tools carry advisories, and no outside penetration test has been done.

SLIDE 18: Honest by design   (about 56 words, 22 seconds; running total 7:28)

Honesty is a feature. A banner on every screen and a sandbox header on every response say this is a sandbox. Any rule not checked against an official source carries a Not verified badge and the line: indicative, confirm with your AD bank. A payment's compliance report is sealed with a hash that the browser recomputes.

SLIDE 19: Why it can work   (about 56 words, 22 seconds; running total 7:50)

Bangladesh Bank data show 881.7 million dollars sent through banks for overseas education last year, a record, up 32 percent. If a comparison took one percentage point off that volume, families would keep about 8.8 million dollars: our arithmetic, not a forecast. We have no partner, pilot or real users yet; these are numbers to test.

SLIDE 20: What comes next   (about 65 words, 26 seconds; running total 8:16)

Next: replace placeholder rules with official Bangladesh Bank sources, partner with an authorised dealer bank for real quotes and KYC, and move the escrow from our simulated chain to a permissioned one. Credit building is in the brief and not built, and we would rather say so. To close: the engine calculates, the AI explains, the human approves, and every step is logged. Thank you.

ANTICIPATED Q&A (keep this page; do not read it aloud)
==========================================================================

Q: Where is the AI? Your models are small and optional.
A: That is the point. The AI's job is to read, explain and propose — not to
   decide. We measured four small models against baselines we ran and
   published the modest results. A bank will not deploy an AI that can
   approve a payment; our architecture makes that impossible by
   construction, not by prompt. The envelope is the contribution.

Q: Why didn't you ship the 13-input risk model that scored higher?
A: Time and re-testing cost, not concealment. It needs five more runtime
   inputs and a bank-review re-test we did not finish before the freeze.
   We published the comparison rather than omit it; it is the first
   improvement on our list.

Q: Are any of your numbers real?
A: Yes, three: the problem framing, the Bangladesh Bank volume figure
   (USD 881.7m, via The Observer) and four study-abroad rules read in the
   official circular. Everything financial — rates, fees, providers, KYC,
   banks, settlement — is synthetic, and the 8.8m figure is our
   arithmetic. Every screen carries the banner.

Q: Why do backend test counts differ between machines?
A: 1,679 backend tests on a machine that has the explainer fixture (1,678 pass, 1 skipped); a fresh clone collects 1,673, runs 1,672 and skips one module whose fixture is excluded by .gitignore. 463 web tests (439 in an earlier pre-merge log) and 24 browser tests.
   The execution evidence report and technical documentation explain the fixture gap.

Q: You have no real users and no partner. Why should this win?
A: Because it is the kind of AI a bank can actually deploy: every number
   from a pure engine, every model behind a deterministic guard, every
   payment approved by a human with a fresh MFA proof bound to the summary
   they saw, and every step in a tamper-evident audit log. That is the
   hard part. A pilot with one AD bank branch and a few dozen families is
   the next step, and we say so.

Q: The live site is a public deployment. Has it been tested?
A: We checked three things on 19 Oct: it answers over HTTPS with a valid
   certificate and an HSTS header, the sandbox banner and X-Sandbox header
   are present, and the comparison matches our test T01. Our attack tools
   ran against lab copies, not that host, and no outside party has tested it.

Q: Why show your security problems on a slide?
A: Because the man-in-the-middle test showed that on plain HTTP a listener
   reads the password and token, and a stolen token can read payments and
   cancel a draft. Approval still needs a fresh MFA proof bound to the
   payment, and that held in every test. The security self-assessment lists
   every record with its raw-log hash. Saying so is cheaper than being found out.
Q: Does the risk score decide anything, and is it accurate?
A: It decides nothing. It orders payments for a person at the bank: its top 1%
   holds 63.2% of the flagged payments in the test period, random order 1.2%.
   As a plain yes or no it is modest: at the desk's rule it sends 0.34% of
   payments to a person, with precision 29% and recall 56%, and flagging nothing
   would already be 99.8% accurate. Precision and recall cannot both pass 90% on
   this data. It learned from public synthetic data, only routes, and every
   payment still needs the parent's fresh MFA code.
